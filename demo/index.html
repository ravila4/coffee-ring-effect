The microscope view. Normally a stain is drawn the way your eye would see it: translucent brown pigment on paper. Dark-field instead mimics the micrographs in Deegan's papers — every deposited particle glows white on black, with no wash — so the fine structure the brown render smooths over, like the scalloped arches inside the rim and the branching veins, stands out. The physics underneath is identical; only the paint changes.

Each stain is a drying puddle of coffee, simulated one particle at a time. The edge of a real puddle sticks to the surface under it rather than shrinking inward as it dries, and water evaporates fastest right at that edge, so liquid flows outward from the middle to replace what is lost. The flow carries the suspended coffee particles with it, and they pile up against the edge and jam there. That is the coffee-ring effect, and this simulation follows the equations Robert Deegan and colleagues worked out for it.

How strong the coffee is, marked φ in the controls above, decides most of what you see. A concentrated drop has enough particles to hold its edge in place until the water is gone, and it dries to a broad dark rim around a speckled middle. A dilute one runs short. Dry patches open along the rim, the ring breaks into a row of scalloped arches, and eventually the edge tears loose and retreats inward, printing a fainter ring wherever it pauses and scattering spokes and stray dots across the center.

A mug ring forms a different way. The liquid sits in a narrow band under the cup's rim, fed by a single drip that ran down the side, so the middle of the footprint stays dry. When that drip wets only part of the rim, the stain comes out as a crescent instead of a closed ring. Setting the cup down hard, or spilling from a height, flings liquid outward, and the rim breaks into fingers with droplets flying off them to land as smaller rings of their own. The smallest of those carry too few particles to build a rim and dry as flat specks.
